NFPA 96 vs. NFPA 17A: Which Kitchen Hood Rules Apply to Your Restaurant

Two NFPA standards, one fire test and one extinguisher standard all touch your cooking line. Here's what each one covers, how St. Louis-area fire codes put them into force, and why hood cleaning and suppression service are separate jobs.

  • Hood, duct & cleaning: NFPA 96
  • Wet chemical systems: NFPA 17A
  • Adopted locally through the IFC

Short answer: NFPA 96 covers the whole kitchen exhaust system (hood, duct, fan, grease cleaning) and requires fire-extinguishing equipment for grease-producing appliances. NFPA 17A is the standard for installing, inspecting, maintaining and recharging the wet chemical suppression system itself. UL 300 is the fire test those systems must pass, and NFPA 10 covers the Class K portable extinguisher.

NFPA 96: The Exhaust System Standard

NFPA 96, "Ventilation Control and Fire Protection of Commercial Cooking Operations," is the broad standard. It covers the hood, grease filters, ductwork and exhaust fan as one system, along with the clearances and construction that keep a grease fire from spreading into the building. It also requires fire-extinguishing equipment for cooking appliances that produce grease-laden vapors.

For owners, the parts of NFPA 96 that come up most are:

  • Grease buildup inspection and cleaning at intervals based on how hard the kitchen cooks (table below).
  • Semiannual service of the suppression system by "properly trained, qualified, and certified person(s)" acceptable to the authority having jurisdiction, including tests of all actuation and control components such as the remote pull station.
  • Fusible link replacement. Metal-alloy fusible links are to be replaced at least every six months under NFPA 17A/NFPA 96 and manufacturer guidance; some code texts set an annual minimum.
  • UL 300 listing for dry or wet chemical systems, plus the rules for when an older system has to be upgraded.
  • Exhaust fan interlock. Recent editions require a means to start the exhaust fan when any appliance under the hood is turned on.

In the current 2024 edition, maintenance is Chapter 12 and mobile and temporary cooking (food trucks) is Chapter 17. Older editions used Chapter 11 for maintenance, so section numbers you see quoted depend on the edition.

NFPA 17A: The Wet Chemical System Standard

NFPA 96 hands the details of each extinguishing system type to its own standard: NFPA 17A for wet chemical, NFPA 17 for dry chemical, NFPA 12 for carbon dioxide and NFPA 13 for sprinklers. Because nearly every modern restaurant system is wet chemical (ANSUL R-102, Amerex KP, Pyro-Chem Kitchen Knight II, Kidde WHDR and similar), NFPA 17A is the one that governs the tank, piping, nozzles and detection over most St. Louis cooking lines.

NFPA 17A sets out:

  • A monthly owner inspection your trained staff can do: system armed, pull station unobstructed, tamper seals intact, service tag in place, no physical damage, gauges in range, nozzle blow-off caps in place.
  • Semiannual maintenance by a trained technician, covering detection, actuation, the fuel shutoff, agent and cartridge checks, and nozzle aim.
  • Hydrostatic testing of wet chemical agent cylinders every 12 years (NFPA 17A-2009 §7.5.1).
  • Keeping appliances in their design position under the nozzles, with a means such as wheel stops or floor markings.

A kitchen fire suppression inspection is where NFPA 96 and NFPA 17A meet in practice: the technician works through the 17A checklist and fills out the tag that NFPA 96 requires, including the fusible links' year of manufacture and installation date.

Where UL 300 and NFPA 10 Fit

UL 300 isn't an installation standard. It's the fire test a pre-engineered system model must pass to be listed for commercial cooking. It took effect in 1994, after vegetable oils and insulated fryers made older dry chemical agents ineffective. Our UL 300 compliance guide covers how to tell if your system qualifies.

NFPA 10 governs portable fire extinguishers. In a kitchen, that means a Class K extinguisher within 30 feet of travel from the cooking hazard, with a placard telling staff to activate the fixed system first. NFPA 96 calls the automatic system the primary protection and the portable extinguisher the secondary backup. See Class K fire extinguisher requirements for the details.

How These Standards Become Law Around St. Louis

NFPA standards aren't laws on their own. They become enforceable when a city, fire district or state adopts a code that references them, usually the International Fire Code (IFC). The IFC's commercial cooking section requires wet chemical systems to be installed per NFPA 17A, requires UL 300 listing, and sets service "at least every six months and after activation of the system." Adopted codes reference specific older editions, so the rule that applies to you is the one in the edition your fire department or district adopted, not necessarily the newest NFPA edition.

NFPA 96 Inspection Frequency for Hoods and Ducts

NFPA 96 schedules inspection of the exhaust system for grease buildup by cooking volume. Cleaning is required when the inspection finds buildup, so in practice cleaning often follows the same rhythm. The IFC hood table uses the same intervals in months.

Type of cooking operationInspect (and clean as needed) at least
Solid fuel (wood or charcoal)Monthly
High volume (cooking 24 hours a day, charbroiling, wok cooking)Quarterly
Moderate volume (most restaurants)Semiannually
Low volume (churches, day camps, seasonal businesses, senior centers)Annually

Hood Cleaning and Suppression Inspection Are Different Services

Owners often assume the hood cleaner "checked the system." They're different jobs under different rules. Hood and duct cleaning falls under NFPA 96's grease inspection and cleaning provisions and the IFC hood section, with cleaning to ANSI/IKECA C10. The cleaner labels the hood with the company name and date and should report any areas it couldn't reach. Suppression service falls under NFPA 96's maintenance rules, NFPA 17A and the IFC suppression section, and ends with a service tag and report from a trained technician.

The two do affect each other. Cleaners must not make suppression components inoperable unless a qualified person is servicing them, and must keep chemicals off fusible links and detectors. ANSUL asks for a suppression inspection immediately after major hood and duct cleaning because "fusible links are wired shut during the cleaning process" at times, which stops the system from operating automatically. Scheduling the suppression service right after cleaning is a simple way to cover that risk.

FAQ

NFPA 96 and NFPA 17A Questions

What is the difference between NFPA 96 and NFPA 17A?

NFPA 96 covers the whole commercial kitchen exhaust system, including hoods, ducts, fans and grease cleaning, and requires suppression for grease-producing appliances. NFPA 17A covers the wet chemical suppression system itself: installation, monthly and semiannual inspection, maintenance and recharge.

How often does NFPA 17A require inspection?

NFPA 17A calls for a monthly inspection that trained staff can perform, plus semiannual maintenance by a trained technician. The IFC adopted around St. Louis also requires service at least every six months and after any discharge.

Which edition of NFPA 96 applies to my restaurant?

The edition referenced by the fire code your fire department or district adopted, which is usually older than the current 2024 edition. The City of St. Louis uses the 2018 IFC, many county and St. Charles County jurisdictions use the 2021 IFC, and Illinois' servicing rule fixes NFPA 96 at the 2021 edition. Ask your local fire official.

Does my hood cleaner inspect the fire suppression system?

Not as part of cleaning. Hood cleaning and suppression service are separate services under different provisions. Schedule a suppression inspection after major hood cleaning, because fusible links are sometimes wired shut during cleaning.

How often do kitchen hoods need to be cleaned under NFPA 96?

NFPA 96 sets inspection intervals and requires cleaning when buildup is found: monthly for solid fuel cooking, quarterly for high-volume cooking, semiannually for moderate volume and annually for low volume operations.
