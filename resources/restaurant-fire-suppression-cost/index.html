Restaurant Fire Suppression Cost: What Drives the Price of Inspections, Recharges, Repairs and New Systems

Why two quotes for the same hood can look so different, what a fair quote should spell out, and how to compare providers without guessing.

  • No made-up price lists
  • Cost factors explained
  • Quote comparison checklist

Short answer: There is no reliable published price list for kitchen hood fire suppression work in St. Louis, so any number you see online is a guess. What you pay depends mostly on how many hoods, appliances, nozzles and agent tanks you have, which brand of system it is, what parts need replacing, and whether the work is routine or follows a discharge. The only accurate price is a written quote from a provider who has seen your system.

Why This Guide Has No Dollar Figures

When we researched this topic, every price range we found came from cost-aggregator blogs and vendor marketing pages, not from an industry association, a government source, or a published survey. The figures also disagreed with each other widely. Repeating them would give you a false anchor going into a conversation with a provider.

What we can give you is the list of things that actually move the price, so you can read a quote, ask the right questions, and compare two quotes on equal terms. If you already know what you need, you can send a request and an independent fire protection provider that serves your area will quote it directly.

What Drives the Cost of a Semiannual Inspection

Kitchen hood suppression systems have to be serviced at least every six months and after any activation, under both the International Fire Code and NFPA 96. The kitchen fire suppression inspection page covers what happens during that visit. The price of the visit usually tracks how much there is to check and replace:

  • Number of systems and hoods. A single hood line with one agent tank is a smaller job than several hoods, multiple tanks, or separate systems for a fryer line and a charbroiler.
  • Number of appliances and nozzles. Each nozzle is checked for the correct type, cleanliness, aim, and an intact blow-off cap. More appliances under the hood means more nozzles and more time.
  • Fusible links. Metal-alloy fusible links are replaced at least every six months under NFPA 96, NFPA 17A and manufacturer guidance; some code texts set an annual minimum. The number of links in your detection line affects parts cost on every visit.
  • Shutoff and interlock testing. The technician tests the gas valve or electrical shutoff, the manual pull station, and any fire alarm or makeup-air interlocks your system is designed with. More interlocks means more to test.
  • Cartridges and cylinder dates. If an expellant cartridge is out of weight, or the agent tank is due for its 12-year hydrostatic test, those items add cost to an otherwise routine visit.
  • System brand and parts availability. Parts for current ANSUL R-102, Amerex KP, Pyro-Chem or Kidde systems are usually easy to source. Older or discontinued systems can be harder to support.
  • Scheduling. Many kitchens want service before opening or after close. Ask whether a provider prices off-hours visits differently.
  • Travel. Some providers bundle travel into the visit; others list a trip charge, especially across the river or out toward Wentzville.

What Drives the Cost of a Recharge After a Discharge

A discharge is a bigger job than a routine visit. The IFC requires service after activation, and the manufacturer's guidance calls for the system to be inspected, recharged, and the cause of the actuation determined. If your system just went off, read what to do after an Ansul system goes off first. Recharge cost usually depends on:

  • Agent volume. How many tanks discharged and how much wet chemical agent has to be replaced.
  • Cartridges and links. Spent expellant cartridges and the fusible links in the detection line are replaced.
  • Piping and nozzles. Lines are cleaned or flushed and nozzles are cleaned and recapped so the system can discharge correctly next time.
  • Cause of the actuation. If a fire or a component failure set it off, there may be repair work on top of the recharge.
  • Timing. A kitchen that wants to reopen quickly may need a visit outside normal hours.

Kitchen cleanup is usually separate from the recharge. Ask whether the provider's quote covers cleaning the hood, plenum and appliances, or whether you'll handle that or hire it out. The fire suppression recharge and repair page explains the service side in more detail.

What Drives the Cost of Repairs and Deficiency Corrections

Repairs are priced by the problem. A missing blow-off cap or a pull station blocked by shelving is minor. A nozzle that no longer matches the appliance below it, a failed gas valve, or an appliance that was swapped without updating the system is a bigger fix. If a fire inspector wrote up the problem, the fire code deficiency repair page explains how corrections are usually documented.

The biggest repair cost driver is appliance changes. Under the IFC and NFPA 96, replacing cooking equipment, moving it, or changing the cooking medium on an existing system means the system has to be brought into compliance. For a system that isn't listed to UL 300, that can mean replacement rather than modification.

What Drives the Cost of a New System or UL 300 Upgrade

New installations have the widest spread because every kitchen is designed separately. The main factors:

FactorWhy it changes the quote
Hood length and number of hoodsDetermines plenum and duct coverage and the number of nozzles.
Appliance mixFryers, charbroilers, woks and griddles each have their own nozzle and flow requirements. Solid-fuel equipment adds more.
Tank count and system typeBigger lines need more agent. Overlapping or zone-style designs cost differently from appliance-specific nozzles but can make future layout changes easier.
Shutoff hardwareGas shutoff valves, electrical shunt or contactor work, and interlocks to the building fire alarm where one is provided.
Replace vs. modifyA pre-UL 300 dry chemical system usually means new tanks, piping and nozzles, not just new agent.
Permits and acceptance testingFire districts and cities set their own fees and tests. Some require witnessing a live wet-chemical discharge, which uses a full charge of agent.
Other tradesElectricians, gas fitters and hood installers may be priced separately.

Local permit rules vary a lot. Metro West Fire Protection District's 2021 IFC amendments require a discharge of the extinguishing agent during acceptance testing, and in the Metro East the Belleville and Edwardsville permits include a witnessed wet function test. The St. Louis County and Metro East Illinois pages explain who reviews hood work in those areas. The kitchen fire suppression installation page covers the design and permit process, and our UL 300 compliance guide explains when an older system has to be upgraded.

What's Usually Included, and What's Often Extra

Providers write quotes differently, so treat this as a list of things to ask about, not a rule:

Inspection of tanks, piping, nozzles and detection lineReplacement cartridges or agent
Test of automatic and manual actuationHydrostatic testing of the agent cylinder
Test of gas or electric shutoffReplacement gas valves or electrical parts

Class K portable extinguisher service and hood and duct cleaning are separate services, sometimes from separate companies. Ask whether they're in the quote.

How to Compare Quotes

  • Get each quote in writing, itemized by labor, parts and fees.
  • Check that each provider counted the same number of hoods, tanks, nozzles and links.
  • Ask whether fusible links are included in the inspection price or billed per link.
  • Ask whether the technician is trained by your system's manufacturer. ANSUL and Amerex both restrict service to distributors they have trained.
  • In Illinois, ask whether the technician holds the state Class 2K license and works for a licensed distributor. Confirm current rules with the Office of the State Fire Marshal.
  • Ask what happens if a deficiency is found: will the provider quote it before fixing it?
  • Ask who sends the inspection certificate to your fire department or district, if yours requires it.

FAQ

Questions About Fire Suppression Costs

How much does a restaurant fire suppression inspection cost in St. Louis?

We don't publish a figure because no reliable local price data exists. The price depends on how many hoods, tanks, nozzles and fusible links you have, the system brand, and whether parts need replacing. A provider who sees your system can give you a written quote; send a request to get one.

How much does it cost to recharge an Ansul system?

It depends on how many agent tanks discharged, how many cartridges and fusible links need replacing, how much line cleaning is needed, and whether the cause of the discharge needs a repair. Kitchen cleanup is often separate, so ask what the quote covers.

Why are new system quotes so different from each other?

Providers may be pricing different designs, different numbers of nozzles and tanks, or a full replacement versus a modification. Permit fees, acceptance testing, gas and electrical shutoff work, and other trades can also be included in one quote and left out of another. Compare itemized quotes line by line.

Is it cheaper to modify my old system than replace it?

Sometimes, but not always allowed. If your system isn't listed to UL 300 and you change appliances, their positions or the cooking oil, NFPA 96 and the IFC call for the system to be brought into compliance, which often means replacement. Your fire department or district has the final say.

Does it cost anything to send a request?

No. Submitting a request is free. The independent provider that contacts you quotes its own work directly to you.
